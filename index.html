Drones are what people call dual-use. The same autopilot and thermal camera that finds a lost hiker at 2am can follow someone home from work. Nobody in McLaughlin 107A decides how every drone ends up being used. But we do decide what our designs make easy, and what they make hard.

The whole module hangs on one idea from risk analyst Stanley Kaplan: before you can judge a risk, you have to say exactly what can go wrong. He called each of those a scenario. This part is about spotting them.

Click through the missions. Every item under “What can go wrong” is a scenario. Notice who benefits and who would get hurt. They're often not the same people.

The responsibility gap

When something goes wrong in a system with lots of hands on it (the designer, the pilot, the code, the regulator), it's easy for everyone to assume it was someone else's job. Owning your piece of it is most of what engineering ethics asks.

This isn't abstract for us

Each of the club's four areas has its own typical scenarios.

Competition

Autonomy under time pressure. What does the aircraft do when the code meets something it wasn't tested on?

Research

Projects like Midair Hotswap, swapping batteries in flight from a mothership. New ideas mean new ways to fail.

Videography

Great shots, and also other people's faces, yards and windows. Privacy lives here.

Outreach

How the public sees drones depends a lot on how clubs like ours fly them.

Privacy is the harm you don't see

Not every scenario is a crash. A camera makes watching people cheap, and the people filmed usually never find out. “Footage of a neighbor's yard ends up online” is a scenario too, and it belongs on the list.

Legal is the bare minimum

The FAA's rules (Part 107 for commercial flying, a separate set for hobbyists) cover line of sight, altitude and flying over people. Follow them, but don't stop there. A flight can be legal and still put someone at risk who never agreed to it.

Designing responsibly mostly comes down to assuming things will go wrong, then building the aircraft so the safe thing happens anyway, even when someone's tired or rushing a test flight.

In Kaplan's terms, a safeguard only does two things. It makes a scenario less likely (l), or it makes it less bad when it happens (x). Safety engineers also rank safeguards by how much they rely on people getting it right. Stronger ones are on the left.

Rule of thumb

If a safeguard only works when someone remembers to do it, it's not a strong safeguard.

StrongestGet rid of the hazardlowers x

Lighter frame, prop guards, smaller props. A crash just can't do much damage.

StrongBuild it inlowers l and x

Geofencing, return-to-home, battery failsafes, backup sensors, a parachute.

OkayHave a procedurelowers l

Preflight checklist, a spotter, flight logs, test-range rules.

WeakestWarn and trainlowers l, a little

Labels, docs, certifications. Helpful, but only if everyone always does the right thing.

Now put the pieces together. In their 1981 paper, Stanley Kaplan and B. John Garrick said risk is the answer to three questions, asked over and over for every scenario you can think of.

s
What can go wrong?
the scenario
l
How likely is it?
the likelihood
x
How bad would it be?
the consequence

Risk is the whole list of those answers, not one number. A list that's missing a scenario gives you a false sense of safety, so the first job is always making the list complete.

Why not just multiply?

Because multiplying can make two totally different situations look the same.

Scenario A
1 × 5 = 5

Rare. A prop shears off over a crowd and someone gets badly hurt.

same score?
Scenario B
5 × 1 = 5

Happens all the time. A hard landing scuffs the landing gear.

Nobody would treat those the same. Keep the scenarios separate so the rare, nasty ones don't get averaged away.

Worked example: Midair Hotswap

Here's how a risk list might start for the club's mothership battery swap.

s · What can go wronglxSafeguard and what it changes
Battery is released before the latch closes and falls34Latch sensor must confirm before release. Lowers l to 1
The two drones collide during docking24Test only over the empty field, people stay 30 m back. Lowers x to 2
Swap fails and the drone runs out of power23Abort and land when the pack hits a reserve voltage. Lowers l to 1

Is it acceptable?

Kaplan's questions tell you what the risks are. They don't tell you whether a risk is okay. That's the ethics part. For each scenario that's left, ask:

ConsentDid the people at risk agree to it? Club members at a test range did. People walking past did not.
FairnessDo the people taking the risk get any of the benefit?
Cost to fixCould you cut it down without much effort? If yes, “acceptable” isn't a good excuse.
